# Automatic topic/motif discovery in historical newspapers and advertisements: DH and media-history practice, epistemology, and representation in editions

Scope note: these notes cover humanities/media-history practice and epistemology only, not the algorithms themselves or LLM methods. The state of knowledge is as of October 2026. Facts about the Welte corpus are taken from the repository (`/home/user/welte-ads/tei/`): 195 `ad*.xml` files; 587 `<seg type="topic">` spans; 183 `<figure>` elements; across all files, 459 `@resp="#claude"` versus 212 `@resp="#np"`; taxonomy `xml:id="topics"` in `tei/shared/encoding.xml`; facsimiles linked as JPG/IIIF `<graphic>`. With ~22,800 tokens over 195 ads, the average ad is about 117 tokens long.

## 1. Precedent projects that discovered topics/motifs in historical newspapers and advertisements, and what they found

### Takeaway
The closest precedent is Melvin Wevers's body of work on Dutch newspaper ads (KB/Delpher). None of it is a "press the button, get topics" study. It is iterative keyword, collocation, time-series and CNN work in which close reading constantly corrected the machine. Its most useful findings are absences (Coca-Cola ads never explicitly say "America") and the finding that the meaning sat in images and implicit cues. Where topic models were run on whole newspapers (Mining the Dispatch, Kölnische Zeitung), they mostly recovered *genres and formulas*, such as fugitive-slave ads, housing ads and insurance ads, rather than abstract themes. I found no computational study of phonograph, gramophone or player-piano advertising.

### Cited Findings
**Wevers & Verhoef, "Coca-Cola: An Icon of the American Way of Life" (DHQ 11.4, 2017)**
- Research question: "Did Coca-Cola advertisements in Dutch newspapers communicate themes that represented the American way of life?" Two explicit workflows: (A) building a sub-corpus with the KB n-gram viewer plus full-text search (Texcavator); (B) analysing it with AntConc (clustering, concordance, collocation, word frequency) — [Wevers & Verhoef 2017](https://dhq-static.digitalhumanities.org/pdf/000338.pdf)
- Unit and scale: the refined query found 2,905 Coca-Cola ads (1890–1990) out of 18,645,511 digitised ads. The analysis used 1928–1967, split into decades (n = 220, 53, 372, 414) — [Wevers & Verhoef 2017](https://dhq-static.digitalhumanities.org/pdf/000338.pdf)
- Segmentation and OCR problems: "the OCR software did not always correctly compartmentalize the advertisements", and classified ads were often merged into one "advertisement". They therefore used a ±10-word "context horizon" so that co-occurrences from neighbouring ads did not count. They dropped the MI collocation score because ads "tend to be relatively short". The n-gram viewer could not separate ads from articles, and a duplicated digitisation of *De Telegraaf* (1970–78) skewed frequencies — [Wevers & Verhoef 2017](https://dhq-static.digitalhumanities.org/pdf/000338.pdf)
- What computation found: an unexpected absence. Within the context horizon, "not a single document" had Coca-Cola co-occurring with a reference to the United States. Close reading of 51 looser co-occurrences confirmed the company "did not explicitly advertise Coca-Cola as an American product", while rival brands such as Royal Crown did ("real American Cola", from 1964). Collocates showed two themes: international/"glocal" character and refreshing taste — [Wevers & Verhoef 2017](https://dhq-static.digitalhumanities.org/pdf/000338.pdf)
- What computation could not see: the authors conclude that any American way of life was conveyed "in implicit terms or through visual content" (depicted modern urban lifestyle, modes of address). Footnotes note lower recall where "Coca-Cola" was part of the image or logo — [Wevers & Verhoef 2017](https://dhq-static.digitalhumanities.org/pdf/000338.pdf)
- Methodological self-description: "working with computational tools is not a linear process but requires going back-and-forth between generating output, interpreting output, and a close reading of the sources" — [Wevers & Verhoef 2017](https://dhq-static.digitalhumanities.org/pdf/000338.pdf)

**Wevers, *Consuming America* (PhD, Utrecht 2017)**
- A data-driven study of the US as a "reference culture" in Dutch discourse on consumer goods, 1890–1990, alternating distant and close reading. It finds the US signified "authenticity, quality, artificiality, superabundance, mechanization, modernity". I did not verify from the full text whether topic modelling was used — [Wevers 2017, Utrecht repository](https://dspace.library.uu.nl/handle/1874/355070)

**Wevers, Gao & Nielbo, "Tracking the Consumption Junction" (DHQ 14.2, 2020)**
- 18,564,411 ads plus 11.5 M articles from *De Tijd* and *De Telegraaf*. The ad/article split comes from OCR metadata, and its accuracy is not reported. The study tracked 265 product keywords and excluded brand names because they sit in logos. Methods were Granger causality and adaptive fractal analysis (Hurst exponent), not topic models — [Wevers, Gao & Nielbo 2020](https://dhq.digitalhumanities.org/vol/14/2/000445/000445.html)
- Findings: ad vocabulary shows "short bursts and fast decay", unlike articles. About 20% of keywords show ads "shaping" articles, 17% "reflecting", 49% complex, 14% none. Ads are framed as a "distorted mirror". The KB gives no OCR-quality metrics, so the authors *assumed* errors were uniformly distributed over time — [Wevers, Gao & Nielbo 2020](https://dhq.digitalhumanities.org/vol/14/2/000445/000445.html)

**Visual ad work by Wevers & Smits (SIAMESE; CNNs)**
- SIAMESE (KB Lab, 2017): a CNN tool that returns visually similar ads for a source ad. It was motivated by the fact that logos and stylised lettering defeat OCR. Its dataset, SIAMESET, holds 426,777 ads from *Algemeen Handelsblad* and *NRC Handelsblad*, 1945–1994 (other KB pages give ~430,000 and 1945–1995). Access is by contract only — [KB Lab SIAMESET](https://lab.kb.nl/dataset/siameset); [KB Lab blog](https://lab.kb.nl/about-us/blog/combining-textual-content-and-non-textual-features-digitized-newspaper-advertisements)
- "The visual digital turn" (DSH 35(1):194–207, online 2019, print 2020). Three uses of CNNs on Dutch newspapers: (1) separating photographs from illustrations, (2) clustering visually similar advertisements, (3) training a network on "visual categories developed by domain experts" — [KNAW record](https://pure.knaw.nl/portal/en/publications/the-visual-digital-turn-using-neural-networks-to-study-historical/); [OUP](https://academic.oup.com/dsh/article/35/1/194/5296356). I saw only the abstract; I could not verify accuracies or how the expert categories were defined.
- Wevers & Smits (ECCV Workshops 2020) asked whether models trained on modern photos transfer to ads from 1950–1995. They report high accuracy for faces, medium type and gender, with gender detection weaker on very small, very large or low-quality faces — [mlanthology record](https://mlanthology.org/eccvw/2020/wevers2020eccvw-detecting); [UvA DARE](https://dare.uva.nl/id/f983a6a1-906a-4987-a3d5-4323efa2b212). A related DH abstract traces gender displays (relative size, position, gaze) in Dutch ads from 1920–1990 — [DH abstracts, "Advertising Gender"](https://dh-abstracts.library.virginia.edu/works/10133)

**Mining the Dispatch (Robert K. Nelson, University of Richmond)**
- LDA topic modelling (MALLET) of ~1,400 issues of the *Richmond Daily Dispatch*, 1861–65 (~112,000 pieces, ~24 M words). The project charted war reportage, court proceedings, "fugitive slave ads" and humour. One reported finding is that fugitive-slave ads were more frequent when the Union Army was near Richmond — [eCampus News press release 2012](https://www.ecampusnews.com/2012/11/13/ur-news-mining-the-dispatch-digital-history-project-uses-text-mining-to-give-new-insight-to-richmond-civil-war-era-newspapers/); [AHA 2012 abstract](https://aha.confex.com/aha/2012/webprogram/Paper8981.html)
- Caveat: the project site (dsl.richmond.edu/dispatch) returned HTTP 503 during this research, so these details come from secondary descriptions. They disagree on the number of topics (37 vs. 40) — [Internet Scout record](https://archives.internetscout.org/r49403/mining_the_dispatch). Nelson's later talk argues topic modelling can show "the concrete political, social, and emotional functions of elusive ideological discourses" (Dispatch vs. *NYT* nationalism) — [IHR Digital History seminar abstract](https://archives.history.ac.uk/ihrcms/podcasts/digital-history/ideology-and-algorithms-uses-nationalism-american-civil-war-and-topic.html)

**Other newspaper topic-model studies in which ads surface as genres**
- Kölnische Zeitung (1803–1945), re-processed and article-segmented, with four topic models compared. The authors report topic modelling "can isolate sections of the newspaper ... like trade registry entries and various kinds of labor market ads". Example topics are insurance ads, housing ads, and garden/farmland leases — [Article-based topic modeling on re-processed historical newspapers (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/400554275_Article_based_topic_modeling_on_re-processed_historical_newspapers)
- Yang, Torget & Mihalcea (2011), topic models on Texas newspapers: pages mixed articles and ads, and a goods vocabulary topic ("worth price black white goods yard silk") emerges — [ACL W11-1513](https://aclanthology.org/W11-1513.pdf)

**Living with Machines (Alan Turing Institute / British Library)**
- "Living Machines: A study of atypical animacy" (Coll Ardanuy et al., COLING 2020) built the first dataset of 19th-century English sentences in which *machines* are labelled animate or inanimate. Detection used an unsupervised BERT-based pipeline, reported as substantially more accurate "especially in highly complex language use" — [arXiv 2005.11140](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11140). It grew out of the LwM "Agency of Machines" strand — [BL repository poster](https://bl.iro.bl.uk/concern/conference_items/8fa5e0ee-dbc1-499d-9623-bccf3e417206)

**Music-technology advertising**
- Targeted searches turned up only digitised primary sources: *Edison Phonograph Monthly*, *Talking Machine World* on Lantern, Trove items, and NYPL clipping files. They turned up no computational study of phonograph, gramophone or player-piano advertising — [Lantern, Edison Phonograph Monthly](https://lantern.mediahist.org/catalog/edisonphonograph06moor_0219); [NYPL R&H clipping files](https://digitalcollections.nypl.org/collections/rodgers-and-hammerstein-archives-of-recorded-sound-clipping-files)

### Inferences
- **Topic models recover genres, not topoi.** In every newspaper precedent, unsupervised topics latched onto *genres and formulas* (fugitive-slave ads, housing ads, insurance ads). The Welte corpus is already a single genre about a single product. A bag-of-words topic model on it would most likely recover (a) language (12 languages), (b) campaign or copy families, i.e. reprints and translations of the same text, and (c) period vocabulary. It would not recover rhetorical topoi such as "Immortality" or "Virtual Presence". This is the main concrete expectation to give the user.
- **Wevers & Verhoef as a template.** Their workflow fits the Welte case better than LDA does. Concordance or collocation around a term (e.g. "Welte", "artist", "living", "lebendig", "levend") and a context-horizon co-occurrence check would *test* the hand topics, especially for absences. Two examples: is "Distinction from Mechanical" ever explicit in the Dutch or Austrian ads, or only in US/UK copy? Is "Immortality" ever stated outright after 1926? A negative result of the Coca-Cola kind ("never explicitly X") is publishable evidence that hand-coding of positives alone cannot give.
- **Pictorial motifs need image evidence.** Wevers & Verhoef put the decisive meaning in the visuals. This supports keeping the pictorial motifs ("Sitting Woman", hostess scenes) and checking them against the images, not only against the prose `<figure><desc>`s.
- **A targeted analogue.** "Atypical animacy" is a close analogue for several Welte topoi ("Phantom Body", "Virtual Presence", "Distinction from Mechanical", the machine as alive or as the artist's body). But LwM *operationalised a predefined concept* rather than discovering it. That is a model for targeted detection of one hand-made topos, not for open-ended discovery.
- **A gap the user can fill.** The absence of computational studies of music-technology ads means the Welte corpus would be a novel case. It is also tiny by these projects' standards (195 ads vs. 2,905 to 18.6 M), so their scale-dependent methods (time-series, LDA) transfer poorly.

### Gaps
- I could not read the full text of Wevers & Smits 2020. I do not know how many expert categories they trained, with what accuracy, or what the "visually similar ads" clusters showed historically.
- The primary Mining the Dispatch pages were unavailable (HTTP 503). The topic count and the exact list of ad-genre topics are unverified.
- I did not find any computational study of patent-medicine advertising, so I cannot report one.
- Europeana Newspapers' own topic or ad work was not researched.

## 2. Multilingual historical newspapers: impresso, NewsEye, Oceanic Exchanges; languages and OCR noise

### Takeaway
The large multilingual projects mostly trained *separate per-language topic models* and treated cross-lingual alignment as an unsolved research problem. Cross-lingual *topic* modelling exists (e.g. ML-DTM on Finnish and Swedish news). However, projects reported that newspapers in different languages are "not translations" of each other, so only similar topics, not matching texts, can be sought. OCR noise was handled by tolerant methods (passim alignment), by restricting periods, or by simply assuming uniform error.

### Cited Findings
- impresso (Swiss/Luxembourg; EPFL, UZH, Lausanne, C²DH) provides corpora, benchmarks, semantic annotations and language models in French, German and Luxembourgish covering ~200 years — [Ehrmann et al., LREC 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.lrec-1.121.pdf)
- impresso topic models were presented separately for the French and German collections — [impresso blog 2019, "Explore TM"](https://api-impresso.uni.lu/documentation/2019/03/05/Explore_TM.html). A 2018 post explains the cross-lingual problem: the *Journal de Genève* of 15.06.1944 "is not a translation of the Neue Zürcher Zeitung ... However, we might find similar topics". Topics are only computed where article segmentation exists — [Ströbel 2018, impresso blog](https://api-impresso.uni.lu/documentation/nlp/2018/09/07/tradingzone-tm.html). (Both pages were found via search; the host failed DNS resolution when I tried to fetch them, so this is search-snippet level.)
- impresso text reuse at scale: 76 newspapers (FR/DE/LB, 1738–2018), ~50 M content items. passim was run first in boilerplate mode, filtering text repeated *within the same newspaper within a one-month window*. Results were 6,177,815 clusters and 16.1 M passages, touching ~17% of content items. Fuzzy alignment detected reuse "despite the presence of (moderate) OCR noise". Stated limits: current tools "do not recognize translation as reuse"; "the lack of a systematic benchmark evaluation is clearly a major limitation"; the user evaluation had only 13 respondents — [Düring et al., Frontiers in Big Data 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10654985/)
- Current status: the impresso site reported the service unavailable after a PHP security advisory dated 21 October 2026 — [impresso-project.ch](https://impresso-project.ch/)
- NewsEye (Austria, Finland, France national libraries; ~15 M pages) built tools for OCR, article segmentation and multilingual content analysis, including "contextualized topic modeling and viewpoint analysis" — [DH abstract](https://dh-abstracts.library.virginia.edu/works/9348); [European Heritage Award](https://europeanheritageawards.eu/winners/newseye-a-digital-investigator-for-historical-newspapers)
- ML-DTM (Zosa & Granroth-Wilding, RANLP 2019) is a multilingual dynamic topic model tested on parallel German–English news and a *comparable* Finnish–Swedish news corpus. It aligns topics across languages comparably to existing multilingual topic models — [ACL Anthology R19-1159](https://preview.aclanthology.org/naacl24-info/R19-1159). A link to NewsEye is not confirmed in the sources I saw.
- Marjanen et al. 2020 applied LDA and dynamic topic models to Finnish newspapers and periodicals, 1854–1917, to study discourse dynamics — [arXiv 2011.10428](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.10428)
- Oceanic Exchanges (Digging into Data; DFG 2017–2021) traced information flow across national and linguistic borders, 1840–1914. It used Chronicling America, Europeana, Trove, KB, the National Library of Finland and Mexico's Hemeroteca. Aims included modelling "the thematic structure of the corpus across languages" and recognising reused texts — [Uni Stuttgart project page](https://www.vis.uni-stuttgart.de/en/projects/dfg-ocex); [DFG GEPRIS](https://gepris.dfg.de/gepris/projekt/329210141)

### Inferences
- **Language will dominate any naive model.** The Welte corpus has the same structure as these projects in miniature: 12 languages across Chronicling America, Delpher, ANNO, BSB, RetroNews and Trove. A monolingual bag-of-words model run over all 12 would cluster by language first. Per-language models would leave most languages with a handful of ads, which is statistically meaningless. So the impresso choice (separate models per language) is not available at Welte scale. Any discovery method must be language-agnostic: multilingual embeddings, or translation to a pivot language, which other researchers cover.
- **OCR noise matters less here.** The Welte texts are edited transcriptions, not raw OCR. The OCR-noise problem that dominates these projects largely disappears. What replaces it is editorial normalisation, which should be documented as a variable.
- **Per-market comparison as a research question.** "Comparable, not parallel" corpora is exactly impresso's framing. The Welte corpus is *partly parallel* (translated copy) and partly comparable (market-specific ads). Distinguishing the two is itself a research result about how Welte localised its rhetoric. Example: does "Home" or "Hostess" appear mainly in US copy, and "Distinction from Mechanical" in German copy?

### Gaps
- I could not access impresso documentation pages (DNS failure) or confirm impresso's topic count, unit and lemmatisation, nor whether any joint FR/DE model was finished.
- I did not verify NewsEye's topic-modelling publications in detail, or Europeana Newspapers' approach.

## 3. Text reuse and circulation (passim, Viral Texts, cross-lingual reuse): reused copy vs. recurring topoi

### Takeaway
Reprint detection, rather than topic modelling, is the most mature newspaper technique, and it suits a corpus built from reprinted and translated copy. Monolingual reuse detection (passim) is robust to OCR noise. As of a 2022/23 *Media History* study, however, "no algorithm can currently detect textual reuse across different languages". Cross-lingual reuse with multilingual embeddings is emerging (2025–26 preprints) but is not established practice for historical newspapers. Media historians warn not to mistake textual similarity for shared meaning.

### Cited Findings
- Viral Texts / passim: clustering based on n-gram shingling and alignment to find reprinted passages inside longer documents in Chronicling America, later in UK, Australian and European papers — [Smith, Cordell & Dillon 2013, "Infectious Texts"](https://ccs.neu.edu/home/dasmith/infect-bighum-2013.pdf); [Smith et al. 2014, "Detecting and Modeling Local Text Reuse"](https://ccs.neu.edu/home/dasmith/infect-dl-2014.pdf); [Viral Texts project page](https://cssh.northeastern.edu/nulab/viral-texts-project/)
- Cordell describes the reprinted material as including "poems, short stories, news, travel accounts, 'how to' pieces, political speeches" — [NEH ODH Q&A](https://www.neh.gov/divisions/odh/featured-project/odh-project-director-qa-ryan-cordell). Cordell (quoted by Keck et al.): "the most viral media are interpretively flexible and highly adaptable rhetorically, aesthetically, politically" — [Keck, Oiva & Fyfe 2023](https://cudan.tlu.ee/pub/2022_Media-History_Keck-Oiva-Fyfe_Transnational-News.pdf)
- Keck, Oiva & Fyfe, "Lajos Kossuth and the Transnational News" (*Media History* 29:3, 2023), an Oceanic Exchanges case study:
  - "while passim works quite well for monolingual datasets, even those with messy OCR, no algorithm can currently detect textual reuse across different languages". They worked around this by building a 668-article hand-picked multilingual corpus and doing collaborative close reading. passim clusters "tended to overrepresent English-language texts" — [Keck, Oiva & Fyfe 2023](https://cudan.tlu.ee/pub/2022_Media-History_Keck-Oiva-Fyfe_Transnational-News.pdf); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/13688804.2022.2146905)
  - Coda: "we should be careful not to mistake textual similarities as evidence for shared understanding". News was "simultaneously international and domesticated" — [Keck, Oiva & Fyfe 2023](https://cudan.tlu.ee/pub/2022_Media-History_Keck-Oiva-Fyfe_Transnational-News.pdf)
- impresso ran passim's boilerplate mode to *remove* text repeated in the same paper within a month before detecting reuse, and states tools "do not recognize translation as reuse" — [Düring et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10654985/)
- Recent cross-lingual reuse work: a 2026 preprint detects sentence-level cross-lingual editorial reuse (Slovenian Press Agency vs. foreign agencies in seven languages) with weak supervision and no full translation — [arXiv 2603.29937](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.29937). Work on adapting multilingual embedding models to *historical* Luxembourgish shows generic encoders need adaptation for historical text — [arXiv 2502.07938](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.07938)
- Visual reuse: "Viral Images" (Buccalon, Sun & Lee, June 2026) embedded 1.5 M Newspaper Navigator photographs with CLIP and clustered them to find reprints. It reports "a diversity of photographs and advertisements" circulating across papers, with a public interface at viral-images.org. The abstract gives no quantitative accuracy — [arXiv 2606.16209](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.16209)

### Inferences
- **Reuse comes first, and the edition already holds it.** For Welte, reuse is the object of study, not noise. impresso's boilerplate filter (same paper, one month) would *discard* exactly the repeated insertions that the Welte edition records as variants. The edition already holds a hand-made reuse graph (works → variants/printings). That is a gold standard against which automatic cross-lingual reuse detection could be tested. The test is small enough to check exhaustively, unlike Oceanic Exchanges, which had to hand-pick 668 articles.
- **Separate reused copy from shared topoi.** The key methodological point for the user is that a recurring motif can recur because (a) the *same copy* was reprinted or translated (one rhetorical decision, counted many times), or (b) *independent copy* re-used the same topos (a genuine topos in the rhetorical sense). Any frequency claim ("Exact Reproduction appears in N% of ads") should be computed over copy families (de-duplicated), not over printings. Machine reuse detection (passim within a language; multilingual sentence embeddings across languages) is the tool that makes this distinction auditable. Concretely: align, for example, the English "as if the artist himself were sitting at the piano" with its German, Dutch and French renderings. A topos that survives translation unchanged indicates centrally produced copy. A topos that appears only in independently written local copy indicates uptake by local agents.
- **Watch the adaptations.** Keck et al.'s "international and domesticated" finding is a direct hypothesis for Welte. Translations may keep the formula but shift the topos, for example dropping "Home" or "Hostess" or adding "Distinction from Mechanical" in some markets. Cross-lingual alignment at span level would show *where* adaptations add or remove motifs.
- **Visual reuse is detectable too.** Following "Viral Images", CLIP-embedding similarity over the ~183 facsimile illustrations could find reused cuts and photos across markets, a visual counterpart of the variant record.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve passim's handling of advertisements specifically (the "Infectious Texts" PDF fetch failed). I could not confirm whether Viral Texts filtered out ads.
- I found no published evaluation of cross-lingual reuse detection on *historical* newspapers or ads as of 2026, only contemporary-news and embedding-adaptation work.

## 4. Epistemology and critique: what topic models "find", and how they relate to hand-built categories

### Takeaway
The DH consensus since 2012 is that topic models output word co-occurrence distributions whose "topic" status is conferred by the interpreter. Three points follow: they cannot validate a theory built into them (Blei); labels mislead unless checked against word assignments and metadata (Schmidt); and without heavy validation they are no better evidence than close reading and invite apophenia and confirmation bias (Shadrova). The constructive positions treat machine output as one stage in an abductive loop with hermeneutic categories: Nelson's computational grounded theory (detect, refine, confirm), Wevers & Verhoef's iterative workflows, and scalable or blended reading. Under that view a hand-made taxonomy is the *model being tested*, not something to be replaced.

### Cited Findings
- **Blei (JDH 2.1, 2012).** "Formally, a topic is a probability distribution over terms." Topic models "discover hidden thematic structure", but "it is still the scholar's job to do the actual interpreting". "A model of texts, built with a particular theory in mind, cannot provide evidence for the theory"; models may "point us to such evidence". The process is cyclical: "She revises and repeats" — [Blei 2012](https://journalofdigitalhumanities.org/2-1/topic-modeling-and-digital-humanities-by-david-m-blei/)
- **Schmidt, "Words Alone" (JDH 2.1, 2012).** Topic models assume coherence and stability over time. In ship logs, one topic merged Hawaiian whaling routes with transatlantic shipping, and the top-ten points hid this. In a *PMLA* model, the topic "grant state twain" split around 1959 into land-grant universities and Mark Twain. Recommendations: inspect word assignments, check stability across metadata, distrust labels, prefer simple transparent methods and more than one method. LDA is "one of many flawed ways that computers can reorganize texts" — [Schmidt 2012](https://journalofdigitalhumanities.org/2-1/words-alone-by-benjamin-m-schmidt/)
- **Rhody (JDH 2.1, 2012).** Topic modelling 4,500 ekphrastic poems: "topic modeling poetry works, in part, because of its failures". Figurative, formulaic language yields topics that are not themes in the ordinary sense — [Rhody 2012](https://journalofdigitalhumanities.org/?p=4083)
- **Schöch, "Topic Modeling Genre" (DHQ 11.2, 2017).** Topics in French drama included abstract themes (love, death), dramatic personnel, settings, concrete activities (letter-writing) and plot-position patterns. Words in a topic need not share "a common abstract theme"; their shared basis may be a setting, a narrative motif, a social group or a register. Choices: ~1,000-word segments, 60 topics selected via classification accuracy (0.87 vs. 0.48 baseline). Caveats: parameter choice involves "intuitive or arbitrary decisions", and fiction "usually yields less abstract topics" — [Schöch 2017](https://dhq.digitalhumanities.org/vol/11/2/000291/000291.html)
- **Jockers, *Macroanalysis* (2013).** 500 "themes" from 3,346 nineteenth-century novels in 1,000-word chunks. "Labels are subjective ... most frequently derived from the topic headwords" — [Jockers, "500 Themes"](https://www.matthewjockers.net/?p=117)
- **Shadrova (*Journal of Data Mining and Digital Humanities* 2021; note: JDMDH, not DSH as the brief assumed).**
  - Topic modelling "does not meet the requirements of an independent research method": it is non-deterministic, cannot be validated against many competing models, has no well-defined linguistic interface, and "does not scholarly model topics in the sense of themes or content". This makes interpretation prone to apophenia and confirmation bias — [Shadrova 2021](https://jdmdh.episciences.org/8608)
  - Category decisions that a topic count cannot encode: granularity, explicitness or implicitness, ambiguity or overlap, scope, and nomothetic vs. idiographic categorisation. Tuning k "until one reaches a satisfactory level of semantic coherence" invites confirmation bias — [Shadrova 2021, PDF](https://jdmdh.episciences.org/8608/pdf)
  - "Topic modeling without massive validation does not provide better, more objective, or more exact evidence to most research questions than close reading would." Interpretation is "abductive", and "naked", "exploratory" results risk becoming "Troyan horses" — [Shadrova 2021, PDF](https://jdmdh.episciences.org/8608/pdf)
  - Research agenda item (d): "How accurate is the topic modeling estimate of topic prevalence compared to the quantification of prevalence based on scholarly modeled topics?" Suggested constructive uses: as a lexical pre-filter (Wendel et al.), and multiscale reading tools such as IRaMuTeQ that keep the link to concrete texts — [Shadrova 2021, PDF](https://jdmdh.episciences.org/8608/pdf)
- **Nelson, "Computational Grounded Theory" (*Sociological Methods & Research* 49(1):3–42, 2020).** Three steps: (1) pattern detection with unsupervised methods and word scores, (2) pattern refinement by interpretive close reading, (3) pattern confirmation with further computational and NLP tests. The aim is to combine expert hermeneutic skill with machine pattern recognition. Replication code is on GitHub — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/somere/v49y2020i1p3-42.html); [UBC profile](https://sociology.ubc.ca/profile/laura-nelson/)
- **Scalable and blended reading.** Mueller's "scalable reading" (moving between close and distant views) is widely cited but its scales are under-theorised — [Krautter 2024 chapter record](https://heibib.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/search/Record/1916788688). Weitin's "Reading at Scale" argues analysts cannot glide smoothly between levels and must choose an abstraction level — [TU Darmstadt](https://www.tu-darmstadt.de/universitaet/aktuelles_meldungen/archiv_2/2017/2017quartal2/index_175936.en.jsp). "Blended reading" (Lemke, Niekler, Schaal, Wiedemann 2015) integrates quantitative and qualitative steps in the Leipzig Corpus Miner — [Datenbank-Spektrum 2015](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s13222-014-0174-x)
- **Practice-level synthesis by Wevers & Verhoef.** They side with those who see computation as "an addition to, not a replacement of" interpretation, citing Hayles's "rapid shuttling" between quantitative information and close reading — [Wevers & Verhoef 2017](https://dhq-static.digitalhumanities.org/pdf/000338.pdf)

### Inferences
- **The Welte "topics" are not topics in the LDA sense.** In topic-model terms the corpus is mono-topical: every ad is "about" the Welte-Mignon. The user's categories are topoi or claims (Exact Reproduction, Immortality, Distinction from Mechanical), i.e. *how* the product is argued. Following Shadrova's parameters, they are fine-grained, often implicit, overlapping, span-local and idiographic. That is precisely the profile topic models handle worst. The honest answer to "is there a more technical way?" is therefore: there are ways to *challenge, extend and audit* the taxonomy, not to replace it with an objectively "discovered" one.
- **Mapping Nelson's three steps onto the project:**
  1. *Detect:* run an unsupervised grouping (clusters of spans or sentences across languages) *without* showing it the taxonomy.
  2. *Refine:* read the clusters against the 33 categories. Look for clusters that match none of them (candidate new topoi, e.g. technical or celebrity-endorsement registers), clusters that merge two hand categories (e.g. "Virtual Presence" + "Phantom Body"), and hand categories split across clusters (e.g. "Exact Reproduction" in a 1905 vs. a 1925 formulation, cf. Schmidt's "grant state twain" split).
  3. *Confirm:* test whether a refined category can be reliably re-identified, by inter-annotator agreement or a held-out classifier, and whether its distribution by year or market survives de-duplication of reprints.
- **Claude's annotations are not independent evidence.** Blei's circularity warning applies directly. 459 of the `@resp` values in the TEI point to `#claude`, against 212 to `#np`. Those assignments were made *with* the user's taxonomy, so they cannot count as independent evidence that the taxonomy fits the corpus. At most they are a second annotator applying the editor's codebook. The editor's 212 assignments are the closest thing to a gold standard. Agreement between `#np` and `#claude` on a shared sample would be the minimal validation Shadrova asks for ("human ratings").
- **A publishable result exists in Shadrova's agenda.** Her item (d), comparing machine-estimated prevalence with prevalence of scholarly-modelled topics, is something the Welte edition is unusually well placed to answer. It has dense span-level expert annotation on a closed corpus.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve Underwood's specific claims (e.g. in "Topic modeling made just simple enough", 2012) and so cannot quote him. I found no explicit published position by a media historian on combining an *edition's* hand taxonomy with machine discovery, beyond the general mixed-methods literature above.

## 5. Topos/motif research and frame analysis as close analogues to "advertising topoi"

### Takeaway
The nearest methodological analogues are not topic models but (a) computational frame analysis in communication studies and (b) motif indexing in folklore. In (a), researcher-defined frames are compared against unsupervised, seeded and supervised methods using a manual gold standard. In (b), a scholarly index such as Thompson's is used as a target schema for locating motifs in text. Both literatures conclude that unsupervised methods find salient, explicit patterns but miss predefined latent categories. Supervised or fine-tuned methods trained on expert annotation work best, and complex or implicitly expressed motifs remain hardest. Computational detection of classical rhetorical *topoi* as such is essentially absent; work on rhetorical figures exists.

### Cited Findings
- **Eisele, Heidenreich, Litvyak & Boomgaarden (*Communication Methods and Measures* 17(3), 2023).** EU coverage in 12 Austrian newspapers, 2009–2019, with a manual gold standard from the Policy Frames codebook. LDA vs. keyword-assisted topic models (keyATM) vs. supervised ML. Supervised ML was best: the top classifier (MLP) reached F1 up to 0.86, and every frame had F1 > 0.7. LDA captured salient, explicit frames (Economic, Political) with "comparably high mean Precision" but "low mean Recall", and "did not find certain frames of interest". keyATM "seems unfit for frame analysis": its keyword-seeded clusters were interpretable but did not fit the gold standard. LDA "cannot distinguish between frames and issues per se" — [Eisele et al. 2023 (UvA PDF)](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/176873306/Capturing_a_News_Frame.pdf); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/19312458.2023.2230560)
- **Topics vs. frames.** Topics indicate "what the text is about", whereas frames describe "how these topics are elaborated and portrayed" — [UCD news on Abdalbaki thesis 2025](https://www.ucd.ie/spire/newsandevents/newsandevents2025/congratulationstosalsabilabdalbakionpassingherviva). Nicholls & Culpepper's comparison of unsupervised frame detection (2020; book chapter 2024 in *Computational Political Communication*) is characterised by Eisele et al. as a systematic comparison of unsupervised approaches — [Eisele et al. 2023](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/176873306/Capturing_a_News_Frame.pdf)
- **LLMs and frames.** A 2025 preprint reports generative LLMs "consistently outperformed by manual coders" for frame analysis and concludes some human validation "was always necessary" — [arXiv 2511.17746](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.17746). This is noted only as context, since LLM methods belong to another researcher's scope.
- **Folklore motif indexing.**
  - Yarlott & Finlayson (CMN 2016) proposed supervised motif extraction with Thompson's index as target. They note one episode ("troll under a bridge") maps to two motif codes, which shows hierarchical and overlapping coding — [Dagstuhl OASIcs](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/OASIcs.CMN.2016.7)
  - Alyami & Finlayson (2026) indexed motifs in the *Arabian Nights*: 2,670 positive examples across 200 motifs; fine-tuned Llama 3 reached F1 0.85 vs. 0.78 few-shot vs. 0.67 fine-tuned embeddings; "complexly expressed" motifs reached only F1 0.73 — [arXiv 2603.19283](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.19283)
  - A Cinderella case study found LLMs useful for spotting variant-specific deviations, which could help *refine* the motif set — [arXiv 2510.18561](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.18561)
- **Rhetorical figures.** Dubremetz & Nivre (2018) detect chiasmus, epanaphora and epiphora with >50% precision — [Frontiers in Digital Humanities](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fdigh.2018.00010/pdf). A 2024 survey names dataset scarcity, language limitations and reliance on rules as the main obstacles — [Kühn, Mitrović & Granitzer, arXiv 2406.16674](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.16674v1). Neither addresses topoi in the classical "commonplace" sense.

### Inferences
- **Frame analysis maps closely onto the Welte case.** The 33 topics function like a codebook of issue-specific frames. Eisele et al.'s result predicts the outcome of an unsupervised run on Welte: it would recover the most salient, lexically explicit motifs (likely "Exact Reproduction", "Home", "Dance/Jazz", "Affordability") with decent precision. It would miss implicit ones ("Phantom Body", "Invisibility", "Immediate Recognizability") and probably mix motifs with "issues" such as product models, prices and dealer addresses.
- **Seed words are not a shortcut.** "Seeding" a model with the user's keywords (keyATM-style) is not guaranteed to reproduce the categories.
- **The best use of the hand annotation is as training and evaluation data.** The 587 annotated spans can (a) evaluate any automatic method, and (b) train or few-shot a span classifier that proposes the 33 topics on new or unannotated spans and variants.
- **What discovery means here.** On the frame/motif-indexing pattern, "discovery" amounts to residual analysis: spans that no category fits well, or that the classifier assigns with low confidence, are candidates for new topoi or for splitting categories.
- **Motif indexing also fits the edition's architecture.** The Thompson-index experience (hierarchical codes; one passage carrying two motifs) supports turning the flat 33-item taxonomy into a hierarchy. TEI `<category>` can nest, e.g. a "Presence of the artist" parent over "Virtual Presence", "Phantom Body" and "Immortality". It also supports allowing multiple `@ana` values per span, which the TEI encoding already permits.
- **Data scale.** The Arabian Nights system needed ~13 positive examples per motif on average (2,670/200). Welte averages ~18 spans per topic (587/33), but some topics will be far below that. Fine-tuning is marginal, and evaluation should be per-topic.

### Gaps
- I found no computational study of *advertising* topoi specifically, and none of classical rhetorical topoi (loci communes) in historical prose.
- I did not read Nicholls & Culpepper's chapter itself.

## 6. Visual side: clustering and zero-shot classification of historical illustrations (CNNs, CLIP, Newspaper Navigator) and feasibility for ~180 images

### Takeaway
Visual DH has moved from CNN feature similarity (SIAMESE, Wevers & Smits 2019/20) to multimodal contrastive models (CLIP) that allow zero-shot labelling of historical images with natural-language category names (Smits & Wevers 2023). Both are documented on Dutch newspapers and ads. At ~180 illustrations, training is out of the question. Zero-shot labelling with the user's pictorial categories, nearest-neighbour search and clustering of embeddings are feasible and cheap, provided results are checked by hand against the known bias of models trained on modern web images.

### Cited Findings
- **Newspaper Navigator (Lee et al. 2020, Library of Congress).** Ran a visual-content recognition model over 16.3 M Chronicling America pages to extract seven classes (photographs, illustrations, maps, comics, editorial cartoons, headlines, advertisements). It was trained on Beyond Words crowdsourced boxes plus extra annotations for headlines and ads, and outputs OCR captions and image embeddings. Data, model and code are public domain — [LoC Signal blog](https://blogs.loc.gov/thesignal/2020/04/newspaper-navigator-surfaces-treasure-trove-of-historic-images-get-a-sneak-peek-at-upcoming-data-jam/); [arXiv 2005.01583 (Lee et al.)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.01583). The September 2020 search app enabled "search by visual similarity" — [LoC press release](https://www.loc.gov/item/prn-20-060/library-of-congress-launches-new-tool-to-search-historical-newspaper-images/2020-09-15/)
- **Arnold & Tilton's "distant viewing"** (DSH 2019; MIT Press 2023) treats extraction of semantic metadata from images as explicitly *interpretive*. The book includes a chapter on movie-poster advertising and colour — [DH abstracts workshop](https://dh-abstracts.library.virginia.edu/works/6262); [NECSUS "On distant viewing"](https://necsus-ejms.org/on-distant-viewing/); [book TOC](https://www.oeaw.ac.at/resources/Record/993695266904498/TOC)
- **Smits & Wevers, "A multimodal turn in Digital Humanities" (DSH 38(3):1267–80, 2023).**
  - Most DH was monomodal, and CLIP enables analysis of image–text combinations at scale. Case studies: interior/exterior labelling of ~42,000 magic-lantern slides; family representation in ~38,000 Dutch children's book illustrations; quick CLIP labelling of historical press photographs, checked by crowd workers, then used to fine-tune CLIP and a scene detector — [OUP](https://academic.oup.com/dsh/article/38/3/1267/7078540) (abstract-level)
  - Zero-shot capability "opens up the way for a multimodal turn", but researchers must be aware of "the specific (historical) bias of multimodal deep learning that stems from biases in the training data" — [OUP](https://academic.oup.com/dsh/article/38/3/1267/7078540) (as quoted in search results)
  - Precursor: Smits & Kestemont, CLIP on magic-lantern slides (CHR 2021) — [dblp](https://dblp1.uni-trier.de/rec/conf/chr/SmitsK21.html)
- **Transfer from modern to historical images.** Models trained on modern photographs worked with "high accuracy" on 1950–1995 ad faces, medium types and gender, but degraded for small or low-quality faces — [Wevers & Smits, ECCV-W 2020](https://mlanthology.org/eccvw/2020/wevers2020eccvw-detecting)
- **Reprinted images.** CLIP embeddings plus clustering find reprinted images and ads among 1.5 M newspaper photographs — [Viral Images, arXiv 2606.16209](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.16209)

### Inferences
- **What the Welte setup allows.** The edition links facsimiles (`<graphic url="ad107-0.jpg">`, plus IIIF URLs, e.g. to Internet Archive scans of *Saturday Night* 1927). So images, not just the editor's prose `<figure><desc>`, are available for an embedding pass. This matters epistemically. Clustering the prose descriptions would mostly re-discover the editor's own vocabulary (circular). Clustering image embeddings gives a semi-independent view.
- **Concrete feasible uses at n ≈ 183:**
  1. *SIAMESE-style "more like this".* For each figure, list nearest visual neighbours across markets. This surfaces reused cuts and photos (the visual counterpart of text variants) and recurring compositions (the woman seated at the instrument, salon or hostess scenes, portraits of pianists).
  2. *Zero-shot audit of the pictorial categories.* Prompt CLIP with each pictorial topic gloss (e.g. "a woman sitting next to a piano", "a portrait photograph of a pianist") and compare its ranking against the 30 figures currently carrying a topic `@ana`. Disagreements are cases to re-read, not errors to accept.
  3. *Unlabelled clusters as candidates.* Images in clusters that match no pictorial category are candidates for new pictorial motifs. Expect the Smits & Wevers bias caveat to apply to 1905–1930 halftones and line drawings.
- **Limits of statistics at this scale.** With ~180 images, distributional claims ("Sitting Woman rises after 1920") rest on very small counts, especially after de-duplicating reused cuts. Visual results support close-looking and finding counter-examples more than quantitative trends.

### Gaps
- I could not verify Newspaper Navigator's per-class accuracy for advertisements, or any published CLIP evaluation specifically on 1900–1930 advertising illustrations.

## 7. Representing machine-derived categories alongside expert annotation (TEI, W3C Web Annotation) and edition precedents

### Takeaway
TEI already has the pieces needed to keep machine-proposed topics distinct from the editor's: `@resp` and `@cert` (and `@source`) on `<interp>`, `<interpGrp>`, `<span>` and `<seg>`; `<certainty>` with a numeric `@degree`; `<application>` in `<appInfo>` to document software; and, since TEI P5 4.x, a standoff `<annotation>` element explicitly modelled on the W3C Web Annotation Data Model, with motivations such as `classifying` and `tagging`. The W3C model itself distinguishes `creator` from `generator` and has a `Software` agent class, but no confidence property. I found no documented edition precedent that encodes *machine-discovered* topic categories next to editorial ones. A documented project convention (ODD) would be needed.

### Cited Findings
- **Lightweight analysis in TEI (AI chapter).**
  - `<interp>` "summarizes a specific interpretative annotation which can be linked to a span of text" and records "the identity of the interpreter". `<interpGrp>` "collects together a set of related interpretations which share responsibility or type" (example: `<interpGrp resp="#TMA" type="structuralunit">`).
  - `@ana` points from text to interpretations. Alternatively, `<linkGrp>`/`<link>` link interpretation and text in standoff fashion. For richer analyses, "more robust TEI mechanisms, based on standoff principles and feature structures, should replace lightweight annotation" — [TEI P5, AI chapter](https://tei-c.org/release/doc/tei-p5-doc/en/html/AI.html)
- **Attribute classes.** `att.interpLike` elements carry `@resp`, `@cert` and `@source` via att.responsibility and att.source — [TEI att.interpLike](https://tei-c.org/Vault/P5/2.6.0/doc/tei-p5-doc/de/html/ref-att.interpLike.html). `@resp` "indicates the agency responsible for the intervention or interpretation" and points to a header entry such as `<respStmt>` — [TEI att.responsibility](https://digitalhumanities.unl.edu/resources/schemas/tei/TEIP5.2.0.2/doc/tei-p5-doc/es/html/ref-att.responsibility.html)
- **`<certainty>`.** It "indicates the degree of certainty associated with some aspect of the text markup". It takes `@cert`, `@degree` (a probability), required `@locus` (name | start | end | location | value), `@target`, `@assertedValue` (an alternative value, to which `@degree` then applies) and `@given` (conditional certainty). Example: `<certainty target="#SYB" locus="name" degree="0.6"/>` — [TEI ref-certainty](https://tei-c.org/release/doc/tei-p5-doc/en/html/ref-certainty.html); [TEI CE chapter](https://digitalhumanities.unl.edu/resources/schemas/tei/TEIP5.2.0.2/doc/tei-p5-doc/de/html/CE.html). Note that `@locus` covers markup aspects. Using `locus="value"` against an `@ana` pointer is a reasonable but not explicitly documented use for interpretive confidence.
- **`<application>`.** It "provides information about an application which has acted upon the document". It sits in `<appInfo>` with required `@ident` and `@version`, and it can `<ptr>` to the parts it acted on — [TEI ref-application](https://tei-c.org/release/doc/tei-p5-doc/en/html/ref-application.html)
- **`<annotation>` (TEI P5, current 4.12.0).**
  - It "represents an annotation following the Web Annotation Data Model". It sits in `<listAnnotation>` within `<standOff>`, with required `@xml:id` and `@target`.
  - `@motivation` takes values from the W3C list: assessing, bookmarking, classifying, commenting, describing, editing, highlighting, identifying, linking, moderating, questioning, replying, tagging.
  - Content model: `respStmt`, `revisionDesc` (`<change who=... when=...>`), `licence`, then body (`note`, `ptr`, `ref`). It carries global `@resp` and `@cert` — [TEI ref-annotation](https://tei-c.org/release/doc/tei-p5-doc/en/html/ref-annotation.html)
- **W3C Web Annotation Data Model.** `creator` is the agent responsible for the annotation or body; `generator` is "the agent responsible for generating the serialization". Agent classes are `Person`, `Organization` and `Software`, the last described as "a software agent, such as a user's client or a machine learning system that creates Annotations". Relevant motivations are `classifying`, `tagging` and `identifying`. There is no confidence or certainty property; one would need an extension — [W3C Web Annotation Data Model](https://www.w3.org/TR/annotation-model/)

### Inferences
- **A concrete encoding pattern for the Welte edition** (a proposal, not an attested precedent):
  1. Keep the editorial taxonomy `xml:id="topics"` untouched as the authoritative codebook.
  2. Add a *separate* `<taxonomy xml:id="topics-machine">` (or `<interpGrp resp="#pipeline-v1" type="discovered">`) for machine-discovered clusters. Each cluster becomes a `<category>` with its top terms or exemplar spans in `<desc>`, and a `@corresp` (or `@sameAs`) to the editorial category it maps onto after refinement. Clusters with no counterpart stay unmapped, which makes the "discovery residue" visible.
  3. Document every model run in `<appInfo><application ident="..." version="...">` (model name, version, date). Point `@resp` at a `<respStmt>` naming the run, so `#claude` (annotation by an assistant applying the codebook) is distinguished from `#pipeline-v1` (unsupervised discovery).
  4. Put machine proposals in standoff `<listAnnotation>` / `<annotation motivation="classifying" target="#topic_3" resp="#pipeline-v1">` with a `<certainty locus="value" degree="0.72" .../>` or `@cert`. Only promote a proposal to inline `@ana` on `<seg>` after editorial review, recording the review as a `<change who="#np">` in the annotation's `<revisionDesc>`.
  5. Export the same records as W3C annotations (`creator.type = Software`, `motivation = classifying`) for interoperability.
- **Why the separation matters.** The edition already mixes `@resp="#np"` and `@resp="#claude"` on inline `<seg type="topic">`. Keeping machine-*discovered* categories in their own taxonomy prevents the problem Shadrova warns about: exploratory output "sneaking" into the authoritative layer.
- **A small encoding inconsistency.** While counting annotations I found one topic span encoded as `<seg corresp="#home" resp="#np" type="topic">` (`tei/ad022.xml`, line 397) instead of `ana="#home"`. Scripts that count `@ana` will miss it.

### Gaps
- I did not find (and so cannot cite) a TEI digital edition that publishes machine-discovered topic categories alongside editorial ones, or TEI-community guidance specifically on encoding LLM-generated annotations. Searches returned only generic TEI responsibility mechanisms.
- I did not verify which TEI release first introduced `<annotation>`/`<listAnnotation>`. The reference page shows it in P5 4.12.0 without a version history.
